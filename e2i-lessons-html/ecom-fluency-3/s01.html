A — Three subscriptions

Claire · Nathan

A marketing lead and her colleague review the tools they pay for. Find all three moves in Nathan's lines.

ClaireWe're paying for three AI tools now. Do we actually need all three?

NathanDepends what each one is. Technically Jasper is a standalone tool, because you open it on its own and it never touches our stack.

ClaireAnd the one inside HubSpot?

NathanThat's an integrated AI feature. The move is to check where the work happens before you compare prices.

ClaireSo which one do we drop?

NathanFor quick subject lines the HubSpot one is fine, but for a full campaign brief I'd double-check whether it can even see last quarter's numbers.

1.Why does Nathan say the two tools aren't the same thing?

Move 1 in action. He classifies before he compares, and that's what lets him answer at all.

2.What does he say to check before comparing prices?

That's move 2 — he names the technique, not the tool.

Understand · listen for the moves

B — Where did 40% come from?

Nathan · Claire

The same two people check an AI-drafted report before it goes to a client. Listen first. Only open the script if you need it.

NathanThe draft quotes a forty percent lift. Where did that come from?

ClaireNowhere. That's a hallucination. It sounds specific, which is exactly why it's dangerous.

NathanSo we stop using it for reports?

ClaireFor brainstorming that's fine, but for anything the client reads I'd double-check every number against the dashboard.

NathanAnd what changes in the prompt?

ClaireThe move is to surface the implied context before you write it. Tell it which quarter, which market, which product line.

Part 2 · 2:55 – 6:00 · context and prompting

Remember · multiple choice

1.3:10In the vegetarian friend example, what makes the context implied rather than stated?

You act on it without being told. The model can't, because nobody told it either.

2.3:50In the pay-rise example, what is the cost of leaving the context out?

Last year's rise, your ranking on the team, the industry average. Without those three the output is advice, not your advice.

3.4:35What is the difference between a one-shot and a few-shot prompt?

A "shot" is an example, not an attempt. That's the trap in option 1, and it's the one most people pick.

Hook first, adjust it, paste it back, then the body. The error never gets a chance to travel.

Pushback lab

18 min

Three statements that are wrong, lazy, or too confident. Disagree with each one using one term from section 2 and one move from section 3. Expect to be interrupted and asked "why?"

Round 1

"The three categories are just marketing. If a tool has AI inside, it's an AI tool. Done."

The category isn't marketing, it's about where the work happens. Technically Gemini in Docs is an integrated AI feature, because you never leave the document, and that changes how you use it. If I want to compare five drafts I go to a standalone tool instead. Same model underneath, different workflow.

Round 2

"You shouldn't have to give AI all that context. If it's actually intelligent, it should work it out."

It can't work out what it was never told. Your colleague can't either — you just forget how much they already know. The move is to surface the implied context before you write the prompt, otherwise you get generic output that would fit any company in the market.

Round 3 — hardest

"Hallucination is just a bug. The next model will fix it, so there's no point worrying about it."

Calling it a bug misses what it is. The same behaviour that invents a fake statistic is what gives you thirty campaign angles in ten seconds. For brainstorming that's fine, but for anything client-facing I'd double-check every number. You don't remove it, you decide which tasks you'll accept it on.
